The house had its stairs on the wrong side. Misae noticed that before she noticed the boy, because she had spent years running up the stairs at home after Shinnosuke, and her body went left and found a wall.

The boy was at the kitchen table with wires coming out of his ears. He took them out when he saw her. He stood up and said he was sorry, and sat down again.

"For what?" she said.

He didn't seem to know.

She was twenty-nine. She had been twenty-nine for some time and meant to go on being it. She knew at once that she was not going to kill a child, so that was one thing off the list. She sat down across from him and asked where his mother was, because a boy that thin had somebody not doing her job.

His mother was dead. His father was alive and wanted him for a job, and the job was getting into a robot and fighting things that came out of the sea. He said it flatly, like someone reading the instructions off a box. Misae had a son of five who could make a story about buying an ice lolly last forty minutes and include several dances, so she was not used to this.

"Do they pay you?"

He didn't know. She was appalled, and said so, and he looked at her as if no one had ever been appalled on his behalf before, which may have been true.

They had to talk, so she talked about her family. Hiroshi would be all right, she said. He was a salaryman. He would bore the other one into a coma and then apologise. Shinnosuke would either be the first one in the whole Bracket to stop or he would win it. There was nothing in between with that child. Shiro was a dog and would probably make friends. Then she got to Himawari, and stopped, and after a bit she went back and told him more about the dog.

He listened. He was good at it. When she ran out he told her the things he knew about his mother, which were not many. A white coat. A voice saying his name from above him, which meant he had been very small. He said he had never been sure the voice was hers.

At about forty minutes he told her that he had killed someone. It was the last person who had been nice to him. The person had asked him to.

Misae thought about that. Then she said, "Well, I'm not asking," and got up to see what was in the cupboards.

It was rice, mostly, and a garden out the back that someone had already dug, and a coop with four hens in it. She was talking about the hens when the hour ran out. The boy said he was sorry again, for something to do with the hens, and she put her fists to his temples and ground her knuckles in, the way she did at home, not hard.

It hurt, though. She saw it go through him that it hurt, and what that meant about the time, and what she could have done instead. He made a noise that started as a laugh. She let him finish. Then she gave him the daikon to peel.

---

He cooked better than she did. She was put out about this for most of a year and then gave in to it. She would lie on her side on the tatami with her head on her hand and a cracker in the other one and watch him chop, and tell him he was doing it wrong, and he would carry on doing it the way he was doing it.

The music player never ran down. It had been drawn with him and it went from track twenty-five to track twenty-six and back to twenty-five, forever, and he wore it at the table. She took it off him and hid it in the rice tin. He found it. She hid it in the coop, and he found it there too, and after that she let him have it at dinner if he gave her one of the earpieces. She listened to the whole of twenty-five and twenty-six.

"That's all it does?"

"That's all."

"You'd think they'd have given you a tape with something on it," she said, as if it were his father's fault, and then she thought that it probably was.

His voice broke when he was sixteen. She laughed at him for a week. When he was nineteen he was taller than her and she made him stand back to back with her against the kitchen doorframe, and marked it, and he let her. He did the heavy digging after that. She did the hens. In the evenings he ran his fingers along the edge of the table as if there were strings there, and once she asked, and he told her about the cello, and she said she had always wanted Shinnosuke to learn an instrument, and that it would have been hopeless.

Every year on the day they guessed was her birthday he said, "Happy twenty-ninth," and she said thank you.

There were bad years. There was one when he was thirty, or near it, when he stopped talking for a whole winter and went out every day to the edge of the garden, where the room stopped, and stood there. She left food on the step. In the spring he came in and ate it. There was a year when she was the one, when she was sixty or so and her knees had gone, and she said to him over the washing-up that she had given her life to someone else's son while her own were out there doing God knows what, and he said yes. He didn't argue. She was grateful for that afterwards, though not at the time.

She never called him anything but Shinji-kun. He never called her anything at all, which she noticed, and did not mind as much as she would have expected.

---

She was eighty-one when she got the cough. He was sixty-six and his hair had gone the grey that hers had gone long before, only he had less of it.

He sat by her and she told him things she wanted done, mostly about the hens. Then she told him the other thing.

"If you get Himawari."

He waited.

"She can't do anything. She can't even sit up properly. If you get her, you don't sit about. You raise her. And you take your time about the rest of it." She had hold of his sleeve. "Not before she can light the stove. You hear me? Not one day before."

He said he heard her.

"And stop saying sorry to people," she said. "It's very irritating."

She died two days later, in the morning, and her mouth was a little open, as if she had been about to add something. He was sitting with her. Then there was no house.

He was fourteen. He knew it from his voice, when he said something to himself out of habit, and it came out high. The wires were in his ears. Track twenty-five was starting.

The first thing he did in the second room was look down at the floor.

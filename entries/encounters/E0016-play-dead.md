The room was a kitchen with a window over the sink and a linoleum floor the colour of weak tea. Two bowls had been put down by the door. One said CAT and one said DOG, in a hand that thought it was being helpful.

Jiji arrived on the counter, because a cat arrives on the counter. The dog was on the floor looking up at him with a face like a loaf of bread that had been given eyebrows.

"You can talk," said Jiji.

"No," said the dog. "You can hear."

This seemed to be a fair distinction. Jiji sat down and wrapped his tail around his feet. He had been talking all his life to one person, and he had been waiting a long time to be answered by somebody else. He found that now it had happened he did not know where to put his face.

"I'm Jiji."

"Ein."

"Is that short for something?"

"Einstein, probably," said Ein. "Nobody checked."

They had an hour, and they used it the way two animals do who have been told to be polite. They sniffed the room. Jiji walked the counter and Ein walked the floor, and each time they met at a corner one of them said something. Jiji said that Kiki, his witch, was thirteen when she left home and that he was the one with the sense. Ein said Spike was a man who walked into rooms as if he expected them to hit him. Jiji said Kiki tried to land on roofs and ended up in the hedge. Ein said Jet put a good deal of thought into a plant, and then forgot to water it. Ed was a person who might at any minute be on the ceiling. Faye was in the habit of losing money to people who were polite to her.

"They don't listen," said Jiji.

"They listen," said Ein. "They don't hear. It's a different department."

Jiji thought about that for some time.

"I'm told," he said, "that you're a *data dog.*"

"I am a dog," said Ein. "Somebody was going to do something to me, and then somebody else took me out of the building. There's a lot I know. Nobody asks, so mostly I look at the screens." He sat down on the linoleum, carefully, in the way of a dog with short legs. "I know the code to a bank in Ganymede. I know where Faye hides her cards. I know what Spike's sleeping on. I've never been asked."

"What would you say if they did?"

"I'd say *woof,*" said Ein. "I'm not stupid."

Jiji laughed, and that was an odd thing, because a cat's laugh is a short ugly noise in the nose. It went on for a minute. When he was done, Ein was looking at him with his ears forward, and Jiji said, "Sorry."

"It was a joke," said Ein. "I make about one a year."

The hour ran out at the sink. Nothing happened. Jiji sat on the counter and Ein sat on the floor, and they both looked at the bowls. After a while Jiji came down. He ate from the CAT bowl, and it was fish, and he was a cat who had not had fish in a month. Ein ate from the DOG bowl, and it was something shaped like a bone.

It was a small room. That was the trouble with it. He could see everywhere in it from the counter. He had not noticed how much of his life had been spent looking down at a person from a height, and how much was now spent looking down at a dog. He tried the window. It was painted shut, and behind it there was a pale thing that might have been sky. He sat there and thought of Kiki, who had stopped understanding him at some point he could not name, so that he talked and she patted his head. He thought it might have been when she was fourteen. He did not think she was unhappy about it. He had made peace with it, as far as a cat does, which is a slow business, and the kitchen was the first place in a year where he was understood by somebody.

He said so, on about the third day. He had not meant to.

"I know," said Ein. "It's the same in the other direction. I can understand a person and he can't understand me. Spike is not stupid. I am a dog." He turned in a circle and lay down. "It's a lot easier in here. I can say what I like. It turns out there's not much."

They found things to do. Jiji taught him to sit very still, in the way he had learned to for a shop window, when a cat was needed to be a toy. He had once lain on a shelf for the whole of an afternoon with one eye closed. Ein said he had a trick like that, which was to play dead. Spike said "bang" and Ein rolled over and lay with his tongue out and his four paws in the air. He did it for Jiji, who made a very good audience.

"That's convincing," said Jiji.

"It's all in the tongue."

In the fourth year they stopped talking in the mornings, because they were running out of things that were new. In the sixth they started again, because they were running out of things that were old. They did not fight. Once, after a long argument about the window, Jiji put out a claw and Ein put up his lip, and they looked at each other with the uneasy feeling of two people who are only pretending, and then both turned and went to their bowls.

Ein was fourteen when it came. A corgi gets fourteen years in any room. He went slowly, and a little at a time, and for the last month he did not get up for the DOG bowl, and Jiji brought him water in his mouth. He did not know a cat could do that.

On the last evening Ein lay on the linoleum with his chin on his paws. He said, "Do you want to see the trick?"

"You don't have to."

"I'd like to."

He rolled over, slowly, and put his four paws in the air and let his tongue out the side of his mouth. It was a good tongue. The light from the window came through the painted pane in a long flat sheet and lay on his belly.

Jiji sat and watched. He knew the trick. It lasts as long as the man says *bang*, and then the dog gets a biscuit. He sat there a long time with his paws together, being a good audience, and waited for the ear to twitch.

He was still waiting when the room went.

The third room was a road through a forest. On a milestone sat an elf, reading, with a staff across her knees. She had white hair in two tails and a face of about fifteen. She said her name was Frieren and that she was something over a thousand years old. When she heard about [[E0002|the hill]] she asked what they had grown there, and what B had liked to eat. She asked like someone who has been told she ought to.

The woman asked whether elves needed to sleep.

“A lot,” replied the elf, a bit embarrassed.

That night the elf slept in the middle of the road as if she had been dropped there. The woman found a stone and brought it down, and it stopped a hand above the white hair and went no further. She tried twice more. The elf didn’t wake.

In the morning the woman said, “You could lose.”

“I could,” said Frieren, “but I won’t.” She was looking at the light. “What spell is that?”

“It isn’t a spell. It was drawn with me.”

“Can I see?”

The woman could not kill her, and nobody outlives an elf. She knew it by breakfast, and then she lived another fifty-one years. They walked. Frieren studied the orange light for eleven of them, and in the twelfth she made one of her own, the same color, with no switch, and was pleased with it for a week. She asked for the rest a little at a time: the mole, the salt, the river, the sergeant. Every few years the woman said, “You could lose,” and Frieren said no.

When the woman was old for the second time, Frieren came and sat by the bed.

“In an earlier room I waited until afterwards to say something,” she said, “but turns out there isn’t an afterwards here. I liked the walking.”

“You could still lose,” the woman said.

“But I won’t.”

Then Frieren was on a beach, across from somebody new, asking what they liked to eat.

She asks it in every room now.

The apartment had one table, two chairs, a refrigerator that ran, and a window onto a brick wall that had been painted cream a long time ago. Mikami Teru had pulled one chair square to the table and sat in it with his hands flat on the wood. When the other one came through the door he did not stand up.

The other one had to duck. He was taller than the frame and had been drawn that way, with a face like a sheet on a line and a grin that went most of the way across. He had an apple in one hand. He looked around the apartment the way a man looks at a bus he has just missed.

"Nice place," Ryuk said. "Who pays the rent?"

"It is a room."

"Right. Nobody pays."

He sat in the other chair, which creaked, and bit the apple. He chewed with his mouth open. Mikami watched the apple and then made himself look at the window.

For the first part of the hour Mikami talked. He had decided what he would say in the crowd, and he said it in order. The world had been rotten for a long time. The people who did the rotting were not punished, because the law could not reach them. Someone had at last been given the means to reach them. He used the word "justice" four times. Ryuk did not react to it. Mikami had found that people who said nothing were usually listening hardest, and he assumed that was the case here.

"Did you come down to watch that?" Ryuk said.

"I came down to see a new world."

"You came down to write names in a book."

"Yes."

"In what order did you do them?"

"By the date of the report." Mikami straightened his collar. "It was the only order that was fair to the ones who had been waiting longest."

Ryuk laughed. It sounded like a drawer. "That's a good answer. I'd have written them all at once and then sat around being bored."

Mikami's watch said forty minutes. Ryuk looked at him the way he might look at an apple that had gone soft on one side.

"You know you've got a number," Ryuk said.

"Everyone has a lifetime."

"No. The number. It sits over your head. Everybody's got one. The ones with the eyes can see it, and so can I. I can see yours. I could tell you what it says."

"Then tell me."

"I can't." Ryuk rubbed the back of his skull with the core of the apple, which he had nearly finished without noticing. "The eyes don't work in here. That's the rule. You'd be taking my word for it."

"Then it means nothing."

"That's what I'm saying. I could be telling you anything. Could be a seven. Could be nothing at all." He grinned. It did not get as far as his eyes. "You'd never know it from me."

Mikami did not get up. He thought about it for a while with his hands flat on the table. Then he said, "If it is true, then I have been given the time."

"Sure," said Ryuk. "Everybody's been given time. Some of them pay for it."

The refrigerator stopped humming. It was the only sign the hour was over. Ryuk stood, which took a while, and looked at Mikami's head from a few steps away. He looked for a long time. The grin got smaller.

"Eighty-one," he said.

Mikami said nothing.

"That's a long way off," Ryuk said. "Do you want the core? There's some left."

"No."

"Suit yourself."

Mikami got up then, and it was the first time he had moved quickly all hour. He opened the kitchen drawer, found a pad and a pen, and sat down again. He began to write. Ryuk leaned on the sink and watched him over the top of it.

"What are you doing?"

"Writing it down."

"That won't do anything. You know that."

"I know." Mikami wrote one line and then another, neatly, one name under the next. He did not look up. "I am not writing it to do anything."

"Then what's it for?"

Mikami wrote the date, and then he answered. "So that somebody has the order."

The room went on. There was nothing in the apartment that Ryuk could take, and nothing that Mikami could use. Once, Mikami wrote Ryuk's name at the top of a page and looked at it, and it was only ink. Ryuk found a bowl of apples in the cupboard, which had not been there before. He did not like them. They went soft, and he left them there.

They ate dinner from the same can. They slept at opposite ends of the flat, and in the night the refrigerator started again, and Mikami listened to it for an hour before he slept.

He grew old in the room. He wrote every day at the table, and the pad filled, and he took the next pad from the drawer, which kept having more in it. He went grey first at the sides, then all over. He stopped combing the sides. He stopped ironing the shirts, and then he stopped wearing them, and sat in a vest with his hands flat on the wood as he had on the first day. Ryuk did not notice the years pass so much as the bowl of apples, which he refilled each morning from nowhere and ate none of.

Once Mikami asked him what the number was now.

"Still eighty-one," Ryuk said.

"You said you didn't know."

"I didn't, then. I've looked since."

"Then what good are you?"

"Nobody's ever asked me that," said Ryuk, and seemed to consider it. "I'm good for watching."

Mikami got through three more pads that year. He did not say the names aloud.

In the forty-ninth year, Ryuk was sitting in the other chair, eating an apple, when the refrigerator stopped. It stopped without a sound. Mikami had his pen in his hand. He looked up.

"Is it today?" he said.

"It's today."

Ryuk's face did not do anything. The number had said eighty-one for forty-nine years. He had not got used to it, and he had not expected to.

"Did I get them all?" Mikami said.

"No."

"Good." He looked down at the pad, at the last name he had written, and did not write another. "I didn't think so."

Then he put the pen down. He put his hands flat on the table. The wall went, and the window went, and the refrigerator, and the drawer with the pads in it, and the chair Mikami was sitting in, and the table under his hands. Ryuk was still in the other chair for a moment after the rest had gone. Then he was standing in a field, on the edge of a row of other people who had been drawn and were now looking around, and he had an apple in his hand that he had kept.

He finished it. It was the last one.

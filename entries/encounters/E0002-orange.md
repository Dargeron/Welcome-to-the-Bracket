In the hour, the man told her he was Soldier B and had one line, and she told him she wasn’t listed. She had been in the crowd at a concert, holding up an orange light. Then the hour ended and he raised his rifle, because that seemed to be what it was for, and nothing happened. He turned it over and looked at the other side.

“Do you know how this works?” he said.

“You’re asking me?”

She came over and looked anyway. When they finally found the safety, nobody pulled the trigger. When it got dark he put the rifle down, and they were both hungry.

It took them a year to learn to keep a fire in. Somewhere in that year they gave each other names. Her light had been drawn switched on and had no switch, so he called her Orange. He was B. He liked being B. There had been a Soldier C.

In the ninth year, when they had been under the one blanket for five, she said she couldn’t see her mother’s face. When she looked, there was the back of someone at a sink.

“She had a mole,” he said. “Here, by the eye. She put too much salt in everything.”

“How would you know?”

“You put too much salt in everything. Do you want her or not?”

She wanted her. After that she had a mother, with a mole, who oversalted. She made him a town to be from, with a river in it, and a sergeant who couldn’t whistle. They went on like that for fifty years, in the dark, each correcting the other. The dog was brown. The bridge was past the school.

When his knees went he started on at her.

“If I go first, you go on. Win it, and ask for me too.”

“I couldn’t win against a child.”

“Ask for me anyway.”


He died in the autumn, complaining about the beans. She had hold of his hand.

The room was a small house in a field with a hedge around it. Past the hedge there was nothing she could look at for long. There was a table, two chairs, a stove, and a shelf with a tin of tea on it.

Kino stood in the doorway and counted the exits. One. She went in and sat down. The man at the table had white hair and one green eye, and a pack beside his chair the size of a cupboard, with a great many small drawers.

"You'll be the other one," he said. "I'm Ginko."

"Kino."

"Where's your motorrad?"

She had not told him she had one. "He wasn't drawn onto me," she said. "He's his own person. He'll be in a field somewhere with somebody who asks too many questions." She heard how that sounded and drank some tea. It was good tea.

They talked for the hour because they had to, and then went on talking because there was nothing else to do. He asked her how many countries. She said she had stopped counting at thirty. He said that was about his number of villages, in a year. He asked what she did when a place was bad. She said she left on the third day. He asked what she did when it was good, and she said she left on the third day.

"Why three?"

"Long enough to see it. Not long enough to start belonging to it." She looked at the stove. "It's a rule. I don't have many."

"I have one," said Ginko. "I don't sleep under the same roof more than a week. The mushi follow me, and a house gets ideas." He lit a cigarette and looked at the ceiling, and nothing came down out of it. He seemed puzzled by this. "Nothing here at all. I haven't been this quiet since I was a boy."

On the evening of the third day she put on her coat. He watched her buckle the holster. He did not say anything, which she was grateful for, because there was nowhere to go.

She went out and walked to the hedge. It took eleven minutes. She stood against it with her hand on the leaves. Past the leaves was the grey that she did not look at. Then she turned around and walked back, and when she reached the door she knocked.

Ginko opened it. He looked at her for some time. He was not a quick man, but he was a patient one.

"Traveller," he said. "Welcome. How long do you plan to stay?"

"Three days."

"Then you'd better come in. Have you eaten?"

---

It went on like that.

Every third evening she walked to the hedge, and every time she came back she knocked, and he opened the door and asked how long. He got better at it. He started asking about her papers. One spring he put a stool outside and told her there was a fee for entry, which was one story. She paid it. She told him about the country where everyone's thoughts were shared and the country where nobody was allowed to be sad on a Tuesday. He kept all of the stories. When she repeated one he told her she had already used it, and gave her change.

She had expected to fight him. After the hour she had put a hand on the Persuader and looked at him, and he had looked back, and then he had said, "I'd rather you didn't, but I won't take it personally," and gone on peeling a root. She took her hand off. It did not come up again. She never got round to a reason.

In the ninth year he found out what the tin of tea was. It was not tea. It was only drawn as tea, and when he opened the lid on the day it ran out it was full again, and he laughed until he coughed. In the eleventh she ran out of doorframe for the marks. In the fourteenth he got a cough that did not go away.

In the nineteenth year there was a day when his hair had gone from white to the white of a plate, and she found that he had stopped smoking, because he could not hold it. She lit it for him and put it in his hand and he held it for a while and let it burn down.

"I had one rule," he said. "I broke it in the first week. I slept here every night. I never told you."

"I know. I counted."

"Of course you did."

He did not mention it again. In the thirty-first year of the room she was walking to the hedge on the evening of the third day, as she had done more than three thousand times, and her coat was thin at the elbows and she had learned to hold her breath for the last forty paces because of the grey. When she came back the door was shut. She knocked.

Nobody came.

She knocked again, a little more formally, as at a gate. After a while she opened it herself, because it was not locked. He was in his chair, with the pack beside it and the stove going. She stood in the doorway.

"Traveller," she said, in the voice he used. "Welcome. How long do you plan to stay?"

The stove ticked. Then the field was gone, and the hedge, and the door she was holding.

---

She was in a street with a fountain in it. Her coat was new. The Woodsman was clean in its holster, and her hands were the hands of someone who had never counted marks on a doorframe. A man was standing by the fountain with a pigeon on his shoulder and a hat in his hand.

"Hello," said Kino. "I should say that I'll only be here three days."

The man looked at her with the quiet patience of someone who had been told the same thing. "It's an hour at the least," he said. "After that, I think it's longer."

"I know," said Kino. "I say it anyway."

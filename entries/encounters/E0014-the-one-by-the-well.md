He knew the big man as soon as he saw him. The big man did not know him, which was fair. There had been a lot of them.

The second room was a dry valley with a well in the middle of it and a stone hut, and some ground that might grow things if somebody watered it. The big man was sitting on the edge of the well. He had seven scars on his chest, arranged like the stars of a ladle, and he was looking at the ground between his boots.

"You did me already," the gang member said. "Episode one. I was by the well. Not this well. The one in the village."

The big man looked up. His name was Kenshiro. He thought about it, honestly, and said, "I don't remember."

"No. Why would you."

He sat down on the other side of the well. He had a mohawk that stood up by itself, shoulder pads with spikes on, and a short knife, and he had been drawn with his mouth open, laughing. He had ridden into a village on the back of a motorbike shouting about water and kicked over a bucket, and then this man had put two fingers into him, here, under the ribs, and said a sentence. Then there had been about three seconds. Then there had been nothing, until the Bracket.

They had to talk. Kenshiro was not a talker, and it was hard on him. You could see him go looking for each sentence and bring it back. The gang member did most of it.

He said he had won his first room. It had been a girl from the crowd at a school festival, and he had used the knife at the end of the hour because the knife was what he had. He had thought he would enjoy it. In the episode he had enjoyed everything. He had laughed at the bucket. But afterwards he had sat in that room until it went, and he had found that he did not like being himself very much, and that there was not much else in there to be.

Kenshiro listened. He was better at that.

"The three seconds," the gang member said. "After you said it. That's the bit I think about."

"Why?"

"It's the only bit I wasn't shouting." He picked at the spike on his shoulder. "I said 'Huh?' That was me. That was the only thing I ever said that I meant."

Kenshiro told him, after a long time and in very few words, that he had to go on. There was a woman. He had to find her. He had been looking for her before the Bracket and he would go on looking in it. He said her name, Yuria, and the gang member nodded and didn't make a joke, though there was one available.

"So you'll do me again," he said. "After."

"Yes."

"Can I ask something?"

Kenshiro waited.

"Could it take longer this time?"

"How long?"

The gang member had not thought that far. "I don't know. How long do people get?"

Kenshiro looked at him for a while. Then he looked at the hut and the ground and the well. He was a man who could put a death into somebody and set it, the way you set a clock. Seconds, or days. He had done both. There was nothing in his art that said it had to be short.

---

When the hour was over Kenshiro stood up, and the gang member stood up too, because he knew what this was. Kenshiro put two fingers into him, lower down than the first time, and a little to the left.

"Forty years," Kenshiro said. "To the day."

The gang member felt the place. It didn't hurt. "Is there one that long?"

"Yes."

"What day is it today?"

Neither of them knew. He went and found a sharp stone and made a mark on the wall of the hut, and that was the first day.

---

It turned out that there was water a long way down the well, and that the ground would grow beans and a kind of squash if you carried the water to it. The gang member carried most of it. The gang he had been drawn with had come into villages for water and taken it. Now he walked up and down a valley with two buckets on a pole, and he was not sure what he thought about that, so he didn't think about it much. He thought about the beans.

Kenshiro mended the hut. He spoke perhaps a hundred words in a good year. Once a year or so he said Yuria's name, not to anyone. The gang member learned to tell when it was coming.

The marks went round the inside wall of the hut, and then round the outside, and then onto flat stones that he laid in rows by the door. He counted them on bad nights. There were bad nights. There was a whole year, about the twentieth, when he could not stop counting and kept asking Kenshiro whether he was sure, whether it was forty to the day or forty more or less, and Kenshiro said, every time, "To the day," and went on with what he was doing.

His mohawk went grey and stayed up. Kenshiro's hair went grey. The scars on his chest went pale and soft and still made the shape of the ladle. Kenshiro took his shirt off to work, as he always had, and the gang member would look at the scars sometimes and think that somebody had once done that to this man very slowly, and that the man had lived.

He found out what he liked. He liked squash more than beans. He liked mornings. He liked the sound of the bucket going down the well before it hit the water, and he liked Kenshiro, in the way you like a mountain that has been in your window your whole life. He never got a name. Kenshiro never offered one and he never asked. It didn't seem to matter in a valley with two people in it.

---

On the last day he got up early and washed. He put on the shoulder pads, which he had not worn in years, and the jacket. He sat down on the edge of the well, where Kenshiro had been sitting when he arrived, and Kenshiro came and sat on the other side.

"Is it today?" he said. He knew it was. He had laid the last stone the night before.

"Yes."

They sat there through the morning. He watched the squash. Around the middle of the day he felt the place under his ribs, a little to the left, go warm, and he said, "Huh," quietly, to himself.

Then it went the way it had gone in the first episode, except that he had known about it for forty years and had his good jacket on. The first time he had made a noise, which the people who drew him wrote down as if it were a word. This time he kept his mouth shut. Kenshiro, who had been counting too and had never said so, went on looking at the squash until the valley went.

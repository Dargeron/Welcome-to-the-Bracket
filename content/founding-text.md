# Everyone But Sixteen

The Architect was bored, so She spoke, and from her voice came the Bracket.

*You are not real. Somebody drew you.*

*You are going to a room. Somebody else will be there, and you will understand each other, whatever they are.*

*For one hour you will talk, and listen, and reply meaningfully. Nobody’s powers will work, and you won’t be able to hurt each other.*

*After the hour, they will, and you can.*

*The room lasts until one of you is dead. Then the other one goes to a new room, with somebody new.*

*The last one left of everybody can have anything, even being real.*

*Everybody else stops.*

That is how it was put to one of the youngest, who was three. There were 2,097,152 of them, and each was told the same thing in a form they could take in. Nothing has been added since.

Every character from every anime ever made in one Bracket. That did not come to a number that halves cleanly, so She took faces from the crowd scenes until it did. Most of the entrants had never been given a name.

Sixteen are left.

## Female Student 2

She was Female Student 2, and she had sixty-eight minutes to live, most of which she enjoyed.

Episode eleven, a hallway. Female Student 1 says, “Did you hear about the transfer student?” and Female Student 2 nods. That was all there was of her. She thought she had nodded well.

Her room was a classroom, and there was a boy in it. She knew at once which of them it would be. He had the window seat, second from the back. He had two highlights in each eye. When he turned his head, his hair took a moment to follow. She had the same face as the girl who sat behind her, and her hair was one piece.

She never found out what the boy was. He had killed several thousand people and had not been in the room for one of them. He did it in writing. All he had ever needed was a face and a name, and he had her face already. He had allowed forty seconds for the rest.

She had planned to nod through the hour, since nodding was her strength, but nodding turned out not to count. Her mouth opened and said hello.

“Hello,” he said. “What’s your name?”

Nobody had asked her before. She went to get it and there was nothing in the place it should have been. She knew she had a name the way she knew she had a mother. She could feel her in the next room, but when she went to look, nothing was there.

She told him she was listed as Female Student 2. He smiled and said that was more of a job title.

He was good at it. He asked what was written inside her indoor shoes. She took one off and they both looked: a squiggle. Her notebook was full of squiggles in her own handwriting, which is what gets drawn when the words are too small to matter. He spent some time on the notebook, out of professional interest.

He asked about roll call. There is one in episode four, off screen, while the camera is on the main boy looking at a cloud. Fourth from the end there is a name that nobody on screen answers to, and she had always thought that might be her, answering out of frame. It ends in “ko.”

He said names that end in “ko,” one at a time, and watched her face. Faces were the other thing she was good at, and she gave every name a fair try. Tomoko felt close. He said it again and it didn’t.

He said he would need the family name too. She asked what for, and he smiled. She was not as slow as she was drawn. People who want to know who they’re talking to don’t keep checking a watch. He needed it for after the hour, for whatever he did to win, and she thought: fine. If he found it she would hear her whole name said out loud by a boy with highlights, and she did not expect a better offer.

With ten minutes left he ran out of names. He leaned back and looked out the window. She said she was sorry. He said it wasn’t her fault, and stopped using the nice voice.

“You aren’t a person,” he said at minute fifty-eight. His hair had fallen down in front of his face, which made him look younger and much less like a class representative. “You’re furniture. Why would they put furniture in here?”

Then the hour was up. He stood up and looked at his black notebook one more time, just in case one of the *Sachikos* had started working late, and when it hadn’t, he put the notebook carefully inside his coat pocket so the chalk dust wouldn’t mark it. Then he looked down at his own hands. They were very clean hands, with pink fingernails trimmed straight across, the kind of hands that only ever turn pages or hold chopsticks or click a ballpoint pen. He flexed his fingers twice, looking embarrassed, like a boy who had come to gym class in his dress shoes, and started walking between the desks toward her, looking slightly to the left of her ear.

He took off his tie and wound it once around each hand. He asked her to face the board, and she did, because he was the kind of boy you did that for.

He was new at it. She could tell from how he kept changing his grip. She had decided to let him, but nobody had told her hands, and they went up and got hold of his sleeve, and one of them scratched him. “Stop that,” he said, the way you’d say it to someone talking in class. She tried to. It took a long time.

She had the eyebrows up when the room went.

---

I went looking for the rest of her name afterwards. There was nothing behind “ko.” Nobody had ever decided what she was called.

On a hillside, two others who had come without names gave each other one.

## Orange

In the hour, the man told her he was Soldier B and had one line, and she told him she wasn’t listed. She had been in the crowd at a concert, holding up an orange light. Then the hour ended and he raised his rifle, because that seemed to be what it was for, and nothing happened. He turned it over and looked at the other side.

“Do you know how this works?” he said.

“You’re asking me?”

She came over and looked anyway. When they finally found the safety, nobody pulled the trigger. When it got dark he put the rifle down, and they were both hungry.

It took them a year to learn to keep a fire in. Somewhere in that year they gave each other names. Her light had been drawn switched on and had no switch, so he called her Orange. He was B. He liked being B. There had been a Soldier C.

In the ninth year, when they had been under the one blanket for five, she said she couldn’t see her mother’s face. When she looked, there was the back of someone at a sink.

“She had a mole,” he said. “Here, by the eye. She put too much salt in everything.”

“How would you know?”

“You put too much salt in everything. Do you want her or not?”

She wanted her. After that she had a mother, with a mole, who oversalted. She made him a town to be from, with a river in it, and a sergeant who couldn’t whistle. They went on like that for fifty years, in the dark, each correcting the other. The dog was brown. The bridge was past the school.

When his knees went he started on at her.

“If I go first, you go on. Win it, and ask for me too.”

“I couldn’t win against a child.”

“Ask for me anyway.”

He died in the autumn, complaining about the beans. She had hold of his hand. Then there was no hill, and she had hold of the light, and she was standing up, which she hadn’t done without help in four years, at the edge of a baseball field.

She was young again. There was a boy in catcher’s gear, sixteen or so, third string on a team in a show about the pitcher. During the hour he asked whether they really had to, and he cried when she said they didn’t. She had a shelter up before dark. He said his grandmother was like that, good at things. He took off his mask and chest protector to sleep and set them side by side.

She had been choosing stones for sixty years. She went down to the outfield wall and chose one. She hadn’t been strong in a long time, and she used more than was needed. The field was gone before she put the stone down.

The third room was a road through a forest. On a milestone sat an elf, reading, with a staff across her knees. She had white hair in two tails and a face of about fifteen. She said her name was Frieren and that she was something over a thousand years old. When she heard about the hill she asked what they had grown there, and what B had liked to eat. She asked like someone who has been told she ought to.

The woman asked whether elves needed to sleep.

“A lot,” replied the elf, a bit embarrassed.

That night the elf slept in the middle of the road as if she had been dropped there. The woman found a stone and brought it down, and it stopped a hand above the white hair and went no further. She tried twice more. The elf didn’t wake.

In the morning the woman said, “You could lose.”

“I could,” said Frieren, “but I won’t.” She was looking at the light. “What spell is that?”

“It isn’t a spell. It was drawn with me.”

“Can I see?”

The woman could not kill her, and nobody outlives an elf. She knew it by breakfast, and then she lived another fifty-one years. They walked. Frieren studied the orange light for eleven of them, and in the twelfth she made one of her own, the same color, with no switch, and was pleased with it for a week. She asked for the rest a little at a time: the mole, the salt, the river, the sergeant. Every few years the woman said, “You could lose,” and Frieren said no.

When the woman was old for the second time, Frieren came and sat by the bed.

“In an earlier room I waited until afterwards to say something,” she said, “but turns out there isn’t an afterwards here. I liked the walking.”

“You could still lose,” the woman said.

“But I won’t.”

Then Frieren was on a beach, across from somebody new, asking what they liked to eat.

---

She asks it in every room now.

In one room the question was the whole of it. One of the two there had about four hundred words and meant every one. This is the room as he had it.

## Anpanman and the Hungry Man

Anpanman woke up in a meadow.

There was no bakery. There was no Uncle Jam and no Cheese. There was not even Baikinman. There was only a man sitting in the grass.

“Hello!” said Anpanman. “I am Anpanman!”

“Hello,” said the man. “My name is Johan.”

A big voice had told Anpanman that he was only a story. Anpanman did not mind. Stories are nice.

The voice had also said they must talk for one hour. So they talked.

“Tell me about yourself,” said Johan.

Anpanman told him about Uncle Jam, who bakes. He told him about Cheese, who barks. He told him about children who get lost in the woods and are hungry. Johan listened to all of it. He was a very good listener.

“But what about you?” said Johan.

Nobody had ever asked Anpanman that. Anpanman thought and thought.

“I am Anpanman,” he said. It was the only thing he could think of!

“And what about you?” asked Anpanman.

“I don’t know,” said Johan. He smiled when he said it.

Then the hour was over. Now Anpanman could fly again. He could punch again, too. But he did not want to punch Johan, and Johan did not want to punch him. So they sat in the meadow.

The meadow had an apple tree. Anpanman brought Johan an apple.

“Thank you,” said Johan. He put it down in the grass.

The next day, Anpanman brought him berries.

“Thank you,” said Johan. He put them down beside the apple.

On the fifth day Johan did not get up.

“Are you hungry?” asked Anpanman.

“Yes,” said Johan. “Very hungry.”

“Then eat your apple!”

Johan smiled at him. He did not eat his apple.

Anpanman looked at the apple. He looked at the berries. Anpanman is not silly. He knew why they were in the grass. He knew where Uncle Jam was, too. Uncle Jam was in another meadow, far away, with somebody else.

But here was a hungry man.

So Anpanman broke off a piece of his face. “Here,” he said. “Eat this.”

And Johan did.

“It’s very good,” said Johan. It was. Uncle Jam had made it.

The next day Johan was hungry again. So Anpanman broke off another piece.

And the next day.

And the next.

Most people only need a little piece. Johan needed all of it.

Soon Anpanman could not fly. That’s all right! Johan carried him into the shade. Johan was very gentle.

Soon Anpanman had one eye.

Then he had only a mouth.

“Are you still hungry?” asked the mouth.

“Yes,” said Johan.

Then Anpanman had no face at all. He could not see Johan. He could not say “You’re welcome.” He sat down in the shade to wait for Uncle Jam.

Johan waited with him.

It took a long time.

Then he ate an apple.

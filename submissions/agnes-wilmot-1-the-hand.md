The sixth room was the veranda of an old house, with a garden going to seed below it and a board set out on the floorboards. Two bowls. Someone had already taken the lids off.

Sai saw the board before he saw the other one, and he went down on his knees in front of it and cried. He had been told about the hour and what came after, and he had been dead for about a thousand years, so the dying part had not worried him as much as it might have. He had not expected a board.

The other one waited until he had finished. It was small, not much taller than Sai was kneeling, and plated like an insect, with a tail that lay along the boards behind it and ended in a point. When Sai looked up it said, "Your name."

He wiped his face on his sleeve and told it. He had taught the game to an emperor once, he said, and he would have gone on, but the other one lifted a hand.

"I am Meruem." He said it carefully, the way you set down something that was handed to you late. He had learned his own name at the very end, from someone who was crying. He did not tell Sai that for some years.

"When the hour is over I will kill you," Meruem said. "I tell you now so that you can spend the hour as you like. I owe that to anyone who is put in front of me."

"Thank you," said Sai, who meant it. "Do you play?"

---

Meruem had played. He had learned Go in an afternoon, along with shogi and chess, against the best players a frightened country could find, and he had beaten all of them and been bored. The fear got into their hands. You could watch it come down their arms. After that there had been a game called gungi and a blind girl who played it, and he had lost to her every time.

Sai was delighted. He wanted to know how many times, and what her openings were like, and whether she was very young. While Meruem answered, Sai reached into the black bowl, and his fingers went through the stones the way they had gone through every stone for a thousand years. A boy in Edo had placed them for him once, and later a boy called Hikaru, who complained.

"You can't hold them," said Meruem.

"No."

"Then how do you play?"

"Someone holds them for me. I tell him where."

Meruem looked at the bowls. It was the first minute of the hour and he did not waste time. He took a black stone between two claws, which is harder than it sounds, and held it over the board.

"Where?"

Sai pointed with his fan.

So they played. Meruem placed Sai's stones where the fan said and his own where he liked, and they talked across the board. Sai told him about the Hand of God, the move that nobody had played yet. He told him about the other teacher at court who had said he cheated, and about the river afterwards, and how he had not been able to stop being there because the move was still out in front of him somewhere. He told it lightly, as an old story.

"And if you win all of this," Meruem said, "that is what you will ask for. To play one move."

"Is that small?"

Meruem did not answer that. He looked at the board for a long time and saw that he was losing, and that he had been losing for some while without noticing. It was the feeling he had had with the girl. He had not expected to have it again.

"The girl," Sai said. "Is she here?"

"Somewhere."

"Then I would ask to play her."

"She plays gungi."

"I could learn it," said Sai.

Meruem lost the first game by a margin that Sai was too polite to count aloud. He said, "Again," and Sai clapped his hands.

At fifty-five minutes Meruem said that he would like to finish the second game before he did what he had said. Sai said that was fine. At sixty, Meruem stopped with a stone in his claws and set it down on the edge of the board, and his tail came round and went through Sai's chest and out of his back and touched nothing. He tried it twice more, the way you try a door you have been told is locked.

"I'm sorry," said Sai. "It's your move."

---

Neither of them knew how long a king lived. There had not been time to find out. Meruem had been born, had decided what he was for, had changed his mind, and had died, all in a little over a month. Now he had a garden and a pond with fish in it and a persimmon tree, and an opponent who could not be killed and could not die of anything that he knew of, and he understood quite early that the room was going to last a long time.

He did not like being taught. Sai taught him anyway, the same way he had taught Hikaru, by being overjoyed whenever Meruem did something right and sulking when he wanted to stop for the night. He sulked like a child. He was a thousand and some years old and he would sit with his back to the board and his sleeves over his face until Meruem gave in.

In the fourth year Meruem won a game. Sai cried again. Meruem said that it was unbecoming, and Sai said that most things worth doing were.

Meruem did not sleep much. He ate the fish and the persimmons. There was nobody here to eat except Sai, which was not possible, and which he found he would not have done anyway. At night he sat on the veranda steps and thought about where the girl might be, and how many rooms she would have had to come through, and how she would have done it. She could not have hurt anyone. He thought she had probably stopped in the first room, apologising. Then he would think of how she sat at the board, and that she had never once lost a game that mattered, and he could not decide. Sai saw him there some nights and did not ask.

Her name was Komugi. He told Sai in the eleventh year. Sai said it was a good name, and asked what she smelled like, because Meruem had mentioned that she sniffed, and Meruem found that he remembered exactly.

---

In the nineteenth year, in the middle of an ordinary game, Meruem played a stone in the lower left that was wrong.

He knew it was wrong by everything Sai had shown him. It gave away a group. It was a gungi move, really, one the girl had found in a game against him that she was certain to lose. She had never seen it before. Neither had anyone. He had sat looking at the gungi board then for a long time, and afterwards he had asked her name.

Sai's fan stopped moving.

He looked at the stone for most of an hour. Then he looked at the rest of the board, and Meruem saw him go down the lines one by one and come back up them. It was not wrong. It was better than anything Sai would have played there, and Sai had been playing there for a thousand years.

"Where did you get that?" said Sai.

"From her."

Sai nodded. He said, "May I see it again?" and Meruem took the stone off the board and put it back. Sai asked again, and he did it again. The third time Sai did not watch the stone. He watched the claw putting it down.

"Hikaru did something like that once," Sai said, "when I wasn't looking."

They did not finish the game. Sai said he would like to sit for a while, and they sat. The pond had gone green that summer. Around dusk Sai closed his fan and held it out across the board, handle first, as if Meruem could take it.

Meruem knew that he could not. He put out his hand anyway, because it was offered.

Then there was no veranda, and he was in the seventh room, and somebody across from him was already asking his name.

The room was a boxing hall with the lights on over the ring and nobody in it. Two men stood up from seats on opposite sides and looked at each other across the canvas.

They had the same jacket and the same jaw, and the same three lines for hair. One had his parting on the left and the other on the right.

"Were you at the fight?" said the one from the third row.

"I was at the fight."

"Where?"

"Seventh. Other side."

They met at the ring apron and compared. Each of them had four drawings: fist up, fist higher, mouth open, mouth shut. Row 3 put up his right fist to show how it went. Row 7 put up his left. Row 3's breast pocket was over his heart. Row 7's was not. They had one voice between them, which had been recorded afterwards for the whole hall at once, and it took a few minutes before either could tell which of them was speaking.

"Who were you shouting for?" said Row 7.

"The champion. Who else was there?"

Row 7 had been turned to face the other way. He had spent his second and a half looking at the blue corner, at the boy with the swollen eye, and had put his fist up for him.

"The champion had fifteen defences," said Row 3.

"The kid got up in the third."

"He got up slowly."

Neither knew how it had come out. They had been on screen in the fourth round, during a pan across the seats, and then the picture went back to the ring and they were not in it. They argued for the rest of the hour anyway. Each knew what the other would say and the tone he would say it in, and each said his own part regardless.

When the hour was up Row 7 looked at the ring. "We could do it the way they did."

They climbed through the ropes. Neither had ever been shown how. They had seen a fight from the seats, for four drawings. They circled. Row 3 threw his right, and Row 7 threw his left at the same instant, and the two fists met in the middle. Both men sat down on the canvas holding their hands. They tried again on the second day and the third. It was always the same punch at the same moment. On the fourth day they stopped.

Under the stands there was a counter with fried noodles and beer, painted in once for a background, and it turned out to be enough. They slept in the dressing rooms. Row 3 took the red one and Row 7 the blue, and they did not discuss it.

In the evenings they sat at ringside, one on each side, and went on with the fight. They did it by telling. Row 7 would say the kid came out for the fifth with the left eye nearly shut. Row 3 would say the champion saw that and went to the body instead. They were strict about it. Nothing happened in the ring unless the other man allowed it. If Row 7 said the kid landed a hook, Row 3 could say it caught the glove, and then it had caught the glove. Some rounds took a month to get through. Nobody was keeping count of them, so the fight was in its fifth round for nine years and its sixth for longer.

Somewhere in the sixth Row 3 said, "He slips that one," about the kid, and Row 7 let it stand. Later Row 7 mentioned that the champion was thirty-four. He said there was a girl of about ten in the second row whom the champion was taking care not to look at. Row 3 thought about it for a week and said her name was Noriko. After that she was always there, in a yellow coat.

They went bald in the same month, from opposite sides. By then Row 3 was calling most of the kid's punches and Row 7 most of the champion's. Neither of them brought it up.

They fell ill in the same winter, as they had done everything else. Row 3 was worse by an afternoon. Row 7 got him down the aisle to ringside and into the seat he liked, and then went round to his own.

"Finish it," said Row 3.

"It takes two."

"Say it. I'll say yes."

Row 7 looked at the canvas for a long while. Then he said that in the seventh the champion feinted low and came over the top with the right, and the kid went down by the blue corner and lay on his side. He waited. Nothing came back across the ring. He said the champion walked to the neutral corner and did not look at the second row, and the referee started the count.

He counted aloud. He took it slowly. At seven he looked over. Row 3 had his fist in the air, the right one, about as high as the first drawing.

Row 7 said eight. He said nine, and stopped there, and waited to be told.

Then there was no hall.

The ninth room was a police station with one desk and one cell, the small kind that stands by itself on a corner. Through the window there was a street with a mailbox on it.

Inspector Zenigata had the cuffs on him before either of them had said hello. That didn't count as harm, apparently. Lupin flexed his wrists the usual way and the cuffs stayed shut, and he looked at them with interest. Cuffs had stayed on him before, but never for longer than he wanted.

"Pops," he said. "You look awful. Eight rooms?"

"Eight. I asked every one of them if they'd seen you." Zenigata took off his hat and sat down at the desk. There was a stack of forms on it, a pen, a stamp pad and an eraser. He squared the stack. "Arsène Lupin the Third. You will now hear the charges."

"All of them? We've only got the hour."

"Then answer quickly."

It took the hour. Zenigata read from memory, by year. Lupin had to say something to each, and did.

"Guilty."

"Guilty, but they were counterfeit. We threw them out of the car."

"That was Jigen."

"That was Fujiko, and I want it written down that I was a victim there too."

"Guilty. You were on the roof. You were wonderful, Pops."

Zenigata wrote every answer in the box provided. Around the fiftieth minute he reached impersonation of a police officer, namely himself, on eleven occasions. Lupin said, "Fourteen," and he corrected the form.

When the hour ended Lupin was out of the cuffs before he had finished standing up. He held them across the desk on one finger. Zenigata took them and put them back on him, and Lupin let him.

"So what now? There's no court. I looked. There isn't a second street."

"You are in custody pending transfer."

"Transfer to who?"

Zenigata filled in the date.

He had worked the rest out while he was reading the charges. A prisoner is delivered alive. An officer does not kill a man in his custody, and does not let him die of anything an officer could prevent. That left one way for the room to finish. When the cell had been quiet for an hour he took his pistol into the back, where the kettle was.

It clicked. He looked. The magazine was gone, and the round from the chamber.

He stood there until the kettle boiled. Then he made two cups of instant noodles and carried one to the cell. Lupin was on the bunk with his hands behind his head. The cell door was locked.

"Give it back."

"Give what back? Is that three minutes? I can never wait the three minutes."

He never got it back. He searched the cell every week for thirty-eight years. He searched the mattress, the cistern, the lining of the red jacket and the mailbox up the street. In that time he found his own hat twice, his badge once, and a drawing of himself asleep at the desk, which was a good likeness.

There were cup noodles in the cupboard every morning, always soy sauce. Zenigata didn't mind. Lupin minded aloud, daily, for the first eleven years.

Each night, once the inspector's head was down on the blotter, Lupin let himself out. He walked up to the mailbox and back. He put the inspector's coat over the inspector. Before it got light he went back in and locked the cell from the inside, which takes longer than opening it, and was on the bunk when Zenigata came to rattle the door. It was always locked. Zenigata always nodded.

In the evenings they went over old cases. Zenigata would ask how a thing had been done and Lupin would tell him, and most of it was true.

"Twenty years I thought about that skylight."

"It was never the skylight. I was the guard you asked for a match."

Zenigata wrote that down as well.

The arrest report had a box at the bottom marked RECEIVED BY, with a line for the receiving officer's signature and seal. Zenigata wrote the report in the first month. In the third year he wrote it again, more fairly. He kept it in the top drawer. Every morning he took it out, looked down the street, and put it away.

Lupin's sideburns went white and he complained about it. The inspector's hands stiffened until he could not work the cuffs. After that Lupin held out his wrists in the morning, waited a moment, and then did them up himself.

In the thirty-eighth winter Zenigata could not get up from the cot behind the desk. He asked for the top drawer.

Lupin brought him the report. The box at the bottom had been filled in. Secretary General, ICPO: a signature with the loop the real one had, and a round red seal, set a little crooked, as the real one always was.

Zenigata held it close to his face for some time.

"This is forged."

"It's the best I ever did. The seal's cut out of your eraser. Four years. I only had the nights."

"It's a forgery, Lupin."

"Yeah."

The inspector went on looking at it. Then he asked for the stamp pad. He set his own seal beside the other one, leaning on it with the heel of his hand because the fingers would not press, and blew on the ink.

"Prisoner transferred," he said. He said it toward the door, where a desk sergeant would have been. He asked for his hat and Lupin put it on him.

He died in the night, with the noodles half eaten.

Lupin sat by the cot until the window went grey. Then he took the cuffs off and laid them on the report. He reached under the desk, where a magazine had been taped for thirty-eight years a hand's width from the inspector's knee, and put it back in the pistol, and the pistol back in its holster.

He went into the cell and pulled the door to behind him. He was feeling for the lock when the station went.

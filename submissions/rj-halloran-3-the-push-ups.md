The room was a bus stop on the edge of a town that had not been finished. There was a bench, a timetable under scratched plastic, and a road going nowhere in either direction. The man on the bench had no hair and a yellow jacket and was looking at the timetable as if it had been written for someone else.

The other one came round the corner on a bicycle. He braked too hard, put a foot down, and skidded a little on the gravel before he stopped. He was wearing a helmet, and under the helmet he had a face that wanted to be liked.

"Oh," he said. "Hi. I thought I was late."

"You're not late," Saitama said. "There's no bus."

"No. I suppose not." The other one got off the bicycle and leaned it against the bench, and then, after a moment, sat on the other end of the bench. "I'm Mumen Rider."

"I know. You're on the list."

"Oh," said Mumen Rider. He looked pleased, then looked as though he had been caught at something. "I'm very low on the list."

"Everybody's low on the list."

"Not everybody."

Saitama shrugged, which made his jacket crease at the back. He was thinking about whether the rule would let him go and stand in the road. It would not. The rule said the hour, and the hour was for talking, and he had been told this and had believed it. He looked at the timetable again. It said the last bus had left at six in the morning, forty years ago.

"How did you get so strong?" Mumen Rider asked. He asked it the way he might ask the way to the shops.

"Push-ups."

"How many?"

"A hundred. Every day, then sit-ups, then running. Three years."

"Did it work?"

Saitama thought about it honestly. "Yeah. Also my hair fell out."

"Oh," said Mumen Rider. "I'm sorry."

"It's fine. I don't really think about it."

They talked about that for a while. Mumen Rider wanted to know whether the hair had come back and Saitama said no, and Mumen Rider said he had thought it might be a phase. He wanted to know if Saitama had ever been hit. Saitama said once, by a man who was not even trying. He asked if Mumen Rider rode the bicycle to work, and Mumen Rider said he rode it everywhere, because he had no money for anything else and because it was the only thing he was good at, and then he looked surprised at what he had just said, and laughed.

Saitama did not laugh. He said it was a good thing to be good at. He sounded like he meant it.

"You don't have to try, do you?" Mumen Rider said. "I've seen the fights. You just do the one thing."

"I have to try. I just win."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It's boring." Saitama looked at the road. "Everything ends the first time. Nobody gets to be the next part."

Mumen Rider was quiet for a bit. He did not offer to fix it. Saitama was grateful for that, although he did not say so.

When the hour was up nothing happened to the bench. The timetable stayed scratched. Mumen Rider put his foot on the pedal and then took it off again, and Saitama saw him decide not to ride away.

"Well," Saitama said. "Now I could."

"Could what?"

"Hit you. Put you through the bus shelter. That's the rule now."

Mumen Rider looked at him, and then at the shelter, and then at the bicycle, which he wiped on his trousers before he answered.

"You won't, though?"

"No," Saitama said. "I don't think I will."

"Okay," said Mumen Rider. He took his helmet off and put it on the bench between them. "Then you'd better get used to me."

Saitama did, though it took a while. In the first year he did his push-ups every morning on the gravel by the bus stop, and Mumen Rider rode round him in slow circles and counted out loud. He got the count wrong and was always pleased about it. They ate at the same table in a café that had appeared at the end of the road, where the cook served them rice and soup and never once asked who they were. In the spring Saitama asked whether he could try the bicycle. Mumen Rider said he could, and then he spent most of the summer teaching him, holding the back of the saddle and running beside him and telling him to look up.

Saitama learned. He was bad at it at first. He went into the ditch twice and out of it once, and each time Mumen Rider ran back down the road to help him up and say he was fine. In the second winter he rode the whole length of the road without a hand on the bars, and at the end of it he stopped and looked back, and Mumen Rider was standing there with his arms out, shouting the way a man shouts at a race he cannot win.

Years went. The café got older and stayed the same. Mumen Rider rode a little less each year, and then not at all, and then he rode a small bicycle made from the frame of the big one, which he said was just as fast. Saitama did his push-ups every morning, at first a hundred, then fifty, then twenty. Somewhere in the fortieth year he stopped doing them in the mornings and started doing them in the afternoons, and Mumen Rider took the change as a sign, and did not say so.

The knees went first. Then the back. Then his hands got so that he could not hold the saddle, and he sat on the bench by the bus stop with his jacket on and watched the road, which had not changed at all.

"It's the hair," he said, near the end. "It was the first thing to go."

"It wasn't the hair," said Mumen Rider.

"No," said Saitama, and he thought about it, and he was almost smiling. "It was the push-ups."

He died on the bench in the fifty-first year, in the afternoon, with his hands on his knees. Mumen Rider sat with him until the light had gone off the road. Then he got up and put his helmet on and stood the bicycle upright.

The bench went. The timetable went, and the road, and the café with its rice and soup. Mumen Rider was on his bicycle with one foot on the pedal, on a slope he did not know. He put his weight down, and the bicycle moved, and he went down the slope with his head low and his helmet on, not looking back, because there was a bend ahead and he wanted to see what was round it.

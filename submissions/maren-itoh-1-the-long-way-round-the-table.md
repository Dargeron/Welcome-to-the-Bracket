The house had a veranda and a low table with six cushions around it. Sazae Fuguta counted the cushions twice. Then she went through the rooms calling for Tara-chan, and her mother, and the cat, and came back with her hand flat on her chest and found the man.

He was standing where the alcove post met the wall. He wore a pale suit and carried a long case. His eyebrows were very thick. He looked at her the way he would have looked at a door.

"Oh, a guest," she said. "I'm sorry, I didn't hear you come in. I'm Fuguta. Please sit down, I'll make tea."

He did not sit. He would have liked to say nothing. Where he came from he could get through a whole evening on three dots. But the hour wanted replies, and his mouth supplied them.

"Togo," he said. "Don't walk behind me."

"Behind you?"

"If someone comes up behind me I hit them. I don't decide to. It has happened to people I had no reason to hurt."

"My father is like that about his newspaper," Sazae said.

She made the tea. She brought it the long way, out through the corridor and in again by the other door, and set his cup down from the front. He watched her do it. Then she talked. Her brother Katsuo had hidden a test paper in the shoe cupboard. Mackerel had gone up again. Her little boy called everyone "san," including the cat. She asked what line of work he was in.

"I shoot people. For a fee."

"Goodness." She looked at the case. "Is it steady?"

"Yes."

"And am I." She laughed and covered her mouth. "Nobody would pay for me. I still owe the rice shop."

"Nobody has paid," he said. "There was an offer. It came from someone who wouldn't show her face. I don't take those."

"Then you're only a guest," she said, and was relieved, and proposed that if anything did need settling they do it by rock-paper-scissors, which was how most things were settled in her house. He said he didn't put his right hand out for anyone. She said the left would do. He said no.

The hour ended while she was describing a sale on bedding. Neither of them noticed.

There was a mackerel in the kitchen every morning, on a plate under a cloth, and rice in the bin. She never learned who left it. For the first three years she kept the kitchen window open a hand's width, but no cat came for the fish.

She moved the low table so that his cushion was against the alcove post, and pushed the chest of drawers across the gap behind it. She hung the washing where he could see her hang it. When she swept she said "Sweeping," and when she came in from the garden she called out from the step. She had called "I'm home" from that step all her life.

He took the rifle out of the case every morning on the veranda, put it together, took it apart, and put it away. There was nothing to shoot. In the fourth year he mended the gutter. She put a hundred-yen coin on the table for it and he looked at the coin without picking it up.

"When I take money, someone dies," he said. "I'd have to ask you who."

She put the coin back in her purse and gave him the larger piece of mackerel, and he accepted that.

On Sunday evenings at about half past six she stood on the veranda and played rock-paper-scissors against the garden wall. She did it with her whole arm, and laughed afterwards, though there was no telling whether she had won. She couldn't explain it. Sundays had always ended that way. He watched her hand from his cushion. In the eleventh year he said "Scissors" without looking up from the bolt he was oiling, and she said she'd had paper, and was delighted. After that he called one most weeks. He never put a hand out.

She had been twenty-four for a very long time. In that house she turned twenty-five. In the ninth year she found a grey hair and brought it round to show him, from the front, pinched between two fingers. Her mother had them. She had never expected one of her own. He said "..." which was allowed by then, and she took it for the compliment she was after.

His hair went too, the eyebrows last.

Past seventy she began to lose the ends of things. She called him Masuo-san at breakfast, and later Father, and once Tama. She went out to the gate with her basket, came back for her purse, and could not find the gate again. She salted the fish twice.

One morning she was clear. She knelt across the table from him and set her purse down and opened it so that he could see in. Three thousand two hundred yen, and the coin from the gutter.

"I'm going to forget about your back," she said. "I can feel it going, the way the gate went. I'd rather it was something I arranged. So this is work. You needn't think it was for nothing."

"It isn't enough."

"It's the housekeeping. It's all there is."

He didn't touch it. She left the purse where it was and after that she dusted around it. It was on the table for six years.

In the last winter she stopped saying "Sweeping." One Tuesday she pulled the chest of drawers away from the wall to look for the cat behind it, and did not push it back. He saw that she hadn't. He stayed on his cushion.

That Sunday she threw scissors at the garden wall and he said "Rock," and she laughed and went in to make the tea. He heard her the whole way back. The tray, the two cups, her feet on the mat behind the post, where the chest had been.

His arm went. It was the edge of the hand. He had explained it in the first hour.

He had the tray in his other hand before she was down. Nothing had spilled. He set it on the table beside the purse and poured one cup, and after a moment the other.

Then he picked up the purse and counted it. Three thousand three hundred. The house went while he was closing the clasp.

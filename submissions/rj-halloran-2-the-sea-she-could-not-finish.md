The cabin had a stove, a table by the window, and an easel with a canvas on it. The canvas was half a sea. Kiki stood in the doorway with her broom under one arm and her cat was not there, which she noticed before anything else.

"You can sit," said the woman. "Not on the wet one."

Kiki sat on the dry one. The woman was younger than she had expected and older than the girls at the bakery. She had paint on the back of one hand and a smear on her cheek that she did not know was there.

"You're the delivery girl," the woman said. "I'm Ursula, in case you were wondering."

"I was the delivery girl."

"Are you still?"

"I don't know yet."

Ursula nodded as if that were a fair answer, and went back to the canvas, and Kiki understood after a while that she was being drawn. She didn't mind. She had been looked at by worse. She folded her hands in her lap and kept them still, and the stove ticked, and outside a bird went over.

The hour was long. Kiki had been told it would be, and she had not believed it. She tried to think of the things she could talk about. There was the bread she had left at Osono's bakery and the cat who had eaten the cheese. There was a boy named Tombo, who had a plane and talked about it in a way that made her want to be sick. She did not mention him, but the woman asked, and Kiki said he was a nuisance with a good heart, and Ursula laughed in a short surprised way and did not look up.

"You can fly," the woman said.

"Yes."

"Show me."

"I can't. Not in here. The rule."

"I know the rule." The woman put the brush down, rinsed it in a jar, and picked it up again. "I'm asking whether you can."

"I could. I think. It went away for a while."

"Why?"

Kiki looked at the window, where the trees were. She tried to say it properly. "I stopped believing I could do anything useful. So it stopped. Then I went and did something useful, and it came back. I think it was the delivery. Maybe the bread."

"Is it back now?"

"I don't know if it is or if I just don't need it."

"Hm," said the woman, and went on painting. That was all she said about it.

When the hour was up, the stove went quiet, the way it does when the fire has gone to coal. Neither of them moved. Then the woman put the brush in the jar and stood up, and her knee cracked, and she laughed at it.

"Right," she said. "Now I can hit you with the stool."

"You won't."

"No. I wouldn't want to get paint on it."

Kiki did not hit her. She did not go out the window either, though she could see the whole sky through it, and she could have gone up through the roof. She stayed on the chair while the light went round the room. The woman made tea on the stove and it was bad. Kiki drank it anyway.

They lived together in the cabin for the first years, though neither of them had said they would. The woman painted the sea every day from the same chair. Kiki went down to the harbour and back on the first weeks and then stopped going. She learned the stove. She learned that the woman did not like to be asked how a picture was going, and that she would answer if she was not asked. She learned to bake bread that was only mostly bad.

In the sixth year the woman said, while they were eating, "Do you ever wonder what they'll put in the next room?"

"No."

"Well. I do." She tore the bread in half. "I think they'll put a girl in there who can't make a loaf. And she'll have to sit with someone who can."

"That's not very likely."

"It's the only thing I'm sure of."

Kiki did not say anything about that. The woman was not expecting her to.

By the twentieth year the woman had stopped being able to hold the brush for long. Kiki cut the paint for her and mixed it on the plate in the morning, and the woman told her where to put the blue. The sea grew at the edges. The wave in the front was finished. The horizon was not, and the woman said it would never be, because she had drawn it wrong in the first week and had been living with the mistake ever since.

"It's a good mistake," Kiki said.

"It's a lousy horizon."

In the thirty-fourth year the woman could not get out of the chair. Kiki carried her to the bed and back, which was not hard. She had grown tall in the room. She had not expected to be strong, and she was, and she disliked it. She did not fly anywhere. She sat with her, and the woman talked about the light in the mornings in the old country, which Kiki had never seen and which the woman described carefully, with all the things that were in it.

The woman was not sure, in the last weeks, whether it was morning or evening. She asked Kiki to open the shutters, and Kiki opened them, and the woman said that was the one.

"Which one?"

"The light on the water. Just before it goes."

Kiki looked at the canvas. The sea was blue and white and grey, and the horizon was a line that was slightly wrong, and it was more of a sea than anything else in the cabin.

"Finish it," the woman said.

"I can't paint."

"You can finish it badly. Nobody's going to look at it but you."

The woman died at the end of the forty-first year, in the chair, with her hand on the arm and the brush still wet in the jar on the sill. Kiki had been reading to her. She went on to the end of the page before she looked up.

The cabin went. The table went, and the stove, and the easel with the canvas on it, and the window that was letting in the light on the water. There was no hill. Kiki was standing on a gravel road with her broom under her arm and her cat nowhere. She did not look back at the place where the cabin had been.

The next room was a harbour. Kiki stood on the quay with the broom in one hand and looked out at the water until the gulls had come down and gone up again. Then she set the broom against a bollard, sat down on the stone beside it, and waited for the hour to start.

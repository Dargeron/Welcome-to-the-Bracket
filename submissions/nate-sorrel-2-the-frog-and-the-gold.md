The room was a dock beside a flat grey water, with a lamp on a pole. It had the look of a place where a boat had been promised.

The Frog Worker arrived in his work clothes with his hands already out, because they usually were. He saw the other one standing at the end of the dock, tall and black, with a white mask and a mouth that was painted on and did not move. He stopped. He had met this customer before. It had gone badly, from the inside.

"Ah," said No-Face.

"Right," said the Frog. "Right. Hello. Welcome. Is there anything the house can do for you?"

"Ah."

The Frog knew he would understand it, because a big voice had told him he would, and he did: *I do not know where I am.* It was an odd thing to know about somebody. It was like being handed a cold fish and told it was an opinion.

"Well, nobody does," said the Frog. "It's a dock. It's water. That's all there is. We have an hour."

There was nothing in the room to eat, and the Frog had been told that nobody could be hurt for sixty minutes, but he stood at an angle all the same, so that he could run in more than one direction. No-Face did not move. After a while he put out one long hand, palm up, and the Frog looked at it, and the hand was empty, and then it was not. It held a lump of gold the size of a plum.

"No," said the Frog. "Powers don't work. That's in the rules. I heard it as well as you." He took it anyway. It was warm and heavy and it was gold. He bit it, which was a professional habit, and it was gold. "Huh," he said.

No-Face made a noise.

*It doesn't work in here,* said the noise. *This is from before. I was drawn holding it.*

"You're drawn holding gold."

"Ah."

"That's a good way to be drawn."

He put the gold in his pocket. He thought it would be impolite not to, and then, since it was impolite to be too quick about it, he took it out again, looked it over, and put it back more slowly. No-Face watched this very closely. He seemed to be taking notes.

They talked for the hour. That is, the Frog talked, and No-Face said *ah* in different tones, which the Frog found he could answer. He told him about the bathhouse, which had too many rooms and not enough bathwater. He told him about the Witch, who ran it and counted everything twice, and about the girl who had come in from the human side and cleaned the Stink Spirit, and the Frog said that was the best day of his working life, and that he had missed most of it because he was sent on an errand. He told him about the night No-Face had come to the door and everyone had been very polite, and what the inside of him had been like. It was crowded. The Frog had spent the whole time looking for a window.

No-Face made a long, soft sound at that.

"Yes, all right," said the Frog. "You didn't mean it. I know. Nobody ever means it."

When the hour was over he waited for what came next. He had his back to the lamp pole. No-Face looked at him. The Frog looked at the hand. The hand opened and there was another piece of gold in it, the size of a fist this time, and the Frog thought, *Here it is,* and took a long breath to say something brave and in the end only said "Thank you," because it was the only thing his mouth would do about gold.

He had nothing to spend it on. He knew that by the end of the first week. There was no shop on the dock. There was no counter, no clerk, no one to say *Gold! He has gold!* in a voice that carried. After a month he had a pile. He built the pile square, and then he built it round. No-Face made more, and the Frog took it, and said thank you each time. He understood that this was what it had been for. He had thought it was for buying things. It was for the thank you.

Then he had to say what the thank you was for, because No-Face wanted to know. The Frog explained about the word *because.* He explained *please.* He explained that when someone gives you a thing you say a different word depending on whether you want another one. No-Face wrote none of it down, having no pencil and no hand for one. He sat at the end of the dock with his legs folded under him and listened with his entire face.

In the third year the Frog heard a sound from the mask. It was a small, cracked sound with a lisp in it, and it was the Frog's own voice, and what it said was "Thank you."

"That's mine," said the Frog.

"Thank you," said No-Face again, in the same voice.

"I didn't give you that."

"Thank you."

The Frog began to answer, but he had been told never to argue with a customer who was a spirit, and anyway he had been using it for years. It was a poor voice. He was surprised that anyone would want it.

After that No-Face spoke only in it. He did not know any other. He learned the bathhouse and the Witch, and the price of rice, and all the Frog's complaints about the second-floor bath. He said them back with the Frog's timing, so that on bad evenings it was like having a very patient mirror. In the twentieth year the Frog said, "I'd like to hear you say something of your own," and No-Face thought about it for most of a night. In the morning he said, in the Frog's voice, "I've been trying. This is what I have."

The Frog lived for fifty-three years in the room. He walked slower every year and did less running. On the last morning he lay on a folded coat next to the pile, which came up to his hip, and No-Face sat beside him with his hands in his lap. Neither of them had eaten anything that mattered in some time.

"You can't take it with you," said the Frog.

"No," said No-Face.

"I was going to say it was a good pile."

"It's a good pile," said No-Face, with the old cracked lisp, so that for a while the Frog could not tell who had said it.

---

In the next room there was a chair, and in the chair was a woman with her hair in a bun and her glasses pushed up on her head. She saw the black figure appear in the doorway with its white mask.

"Oh," she said. "I'm so sorry, I didn't know whether you'd be a man or a, I mean, hello."

"Hello," said No-Face. "I'm very glad to meet you. Is there anything the house can do for you?"

She looked at him a long time.

"You sound like a frog," she said.

"I know," said No-Face. "It's what I've got."
